\section{Introduction \& Motivation}
\label{sec:introduction}

A phone stops working and goes in for repair. Its owner keeps the messaging
account running on a spare handset, and the conversations continue there for
as long as the repair takes. The original phone comes back from repair with
its own history intact. Its owner now holds two archives of one account: the
repaired phone has everything up to the failure, the spare has everything
since, and no operation on either device produces the union of the two.
Restoring a backup onto the repaired phone does not append the repair window
to it, it substitutes one state for the other.

Nothing was deleted. Both archives are intact, each on the device that
accumulated it, and each holds messages the other lacks. What is absent is not
data but an operation: we found no documented way to put the two histories
together. WhatsApp's own restore guidance requires the same account that created
the backup~\citep{whatsapp_backup_restore}; it documents no operation for
combining two backups, a gap that has motivated the third-party merge tools
surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:related:tooling}.

The distinction between an undocumented operation and an absent one is worth
holding onto, because it is the difference between a claim we can support and one
we cannot. What we establish is that the documentation describes no such
operation and that a body of third-party tools exists to supply what it describes
no way of doing --- which is evidence of a gap in what users can reach, not proof
that no path exists anywhere in the product. We have run no systematic enumeration
of the platform's export, restore, transfer and migration affordances, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations} records that as an outstanding gap rather
than resolving it here.

We call this state \textbf{Temporal Communication Fragmentation} (TCF). Its
signature is intact data that no platform operation will present as a single
history. In the latent case both archives remain readable on their own devices
and only the combined view is unavailable; a restore that replaces rather than
merges makes the fragmentation manifest, leaving one archive intact but no
longer surfaced at all. In either case the data survives and the affordance to
reach it as a whole does not.

Three properties make the situation harder than it first appears. The two
archives may overlap in part as well as diverge, so neither is in general a
superset of the other. They carry no shared version history, no operation log,
and no common ancestor, because consumer chat exports were never designed to be
reconciled.
And the platform paths that do bring the two archives back together, restore
above all, give no warning that a competing archive exists, attempt no
comparison, and report nothing about what was replaced.

\paragraph{Motivation, stated as motivation.}
This work began with the author holding two simultaneously preserved
portions of one WhatsApp history: a primary handset unavailable for several
months, the account carried on a spare handset meanwhile, and the primary
returned from repair with its earlier history still present. Neither portion
had been deleted and no restore was performed; the difficulty was that no
available operation would combine them. That situation prompted the research
questions below. It is an anecdote. It is not evidence, no controlled experiment
was run against it, and no claim in this paper rests on it.

The paper asks three questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] What conditions must hold for a single account's message history
to end up distributed across two or more independently preserved archives,
each internally complete, that the platform provides no operation to
reconcile into one history?
  \item[RQ2.] Given two or more such archives, what does a reconstruction
    procedure have to guarantee for its output to be trustworthy as a record,
    rather than merely plausible?
  \item[RQ3.] Where do those guarantees sit relative to what optimistic
    replication, digital forensics, and the existing body of chat-merging tools
    already provide?
\end{description}

Answering RQ1 gives a formal characterisation of TCF with three necessary
conditions (Section~\ref{sec:tcf}). Answering RQ2 gives CARTA (Chat Archive
Reconstruction \& Temporal Access), a platform-agnostic specification of a
three-stage reconstruction contract, \carta, in which the data passed between
stages is typed, records that cannot be safely interpreted are retained in a
quarantine set $\mathcal{Q}$ rather than dropped, and every reconstruction emits
a structured report $\Omega$ covering provenance, quarantine carryover,
conflicts, and counts (Sections~\ref{sec:carta} and~\ref{sec:algorithms}).
Answering RQ3 is Section~\ref{sec:related}.

\paragraph{What this paper does and does not do.}
The contribution is a problem definition and a specification, accompanied by
a reference implementation that exercises it. CHAMP-Pipeline, the
WhatsApp-specific reference implementation of CARTA, is described in
Section~\ref{sec:implementation} and evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} against synthetic fragment pairs whose ground
truth is known by construction; that evaluation establishes that the
specification is implementable and reproduces a known timeline, and nothing
about real archives. No real WhatsApp export has been reconstructed, no
accuracy, precision or recall figure is reported, and no performance
measurement is made, for the reasons Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:limits} sets
out. Where a capability is stated, it is either exhibited by the reference
implementation on synthetic input, required by the specification of a
conforming implementation, or attributed to someone else's system.
